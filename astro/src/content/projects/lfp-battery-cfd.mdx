## Objective

This study examines the thermal behavior of a commercial 280Ah Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP) battery cell under natural convection. The goal is to determine the maximum steady-state temperature that a single cell, as well as a stack of multiple cells, reaches in a stationary ambient environment without active cooling. Establishing this baseline is necessary before introducing forced convection or liquid cooling in future analyses.

## Studies Overview

Two studies were conducted to investigate the cell's thermal behavior under different conditions:

- **Study #1:** A single battery cell within a small computational domain.
- **Study #2:** 100 cells arranged in five vertically stacked layers, each layer containing two parallel rows of ten cells. Within each layer, the two rows are separated by a 3 mm gap, and adjacent layers are separated by a 5 mm gap, within a properly sized computational domain.

Both studies were simulated under natural convection at 1 atm and 25°C ambient.

## Battery Specifications and Heat Generation

### Cell

The model is based on the REPT Battero 280Ah prismatic LFP cell, with a nominal voltage of 3.2V and dimensions of 71.7 × 174 × 206.8 mm. The manufacturer's datasheet recommends a 0.5C discharge rate for maximizing cycle life, and this rate was selected as the operating condition for the simulation.

The thermal conductivity was deliberately set to the through-plane value of 1 W/m·K and applied isotropically. Real LFP cells are anisotropic, conducting heat much more efficiently along their electrode layers than across them. By assuming the lower value in all directions, heat is forced to spread more slowly through the cell, which leads to an over-prediction of internal temperatures.

### Heat Generation Calculation

At 0.5C discharge, the current flowing through each cell is:

$I = 0.5 \times 280 \ \text{Ah} = 140 \ \text{A}$

Applying Joule's law for ohmic heating:

$P = I^2 \times R = (140)^2 \times 0.0002 = 3.92 \ \text{W per cell}$

This value was applied as a constant volumetric heat source on each cell in the simulation.

## Simulation Setup

The simulations were performed in SolidWorks 2025 Flow Simulation using an external, steady-state analysis. Fluid flow, conduction through solids, and gravity (in the −Y direction) were all enabled, with air as the working fluid. The outer faces of the computational domain were defined as open boundaries at 25°C and 1 atm, allowing air to enter and exit freely. Any solid bodies without an assigned material were treated as adiabatic. Initial conditions were set to 25°C, 1 atm, and zero velocity throughout the domain. The global mesh was set to Level 4 with no local refinement, and maximum and average solid temperatures were monitored as convergence goals.

Computational domain sizes differ between the two studies. Study #1 used 0.373 m × 0.407 m × 0.271 m (X × Y × Z), which was intentionally undersized as an initial draft. Study #2 followed published recommendations for natural convection domains, with clearance of approximately three times the rack height above, two times the module width on each side, and one times the rack height below, resulting in a domain of 2.075 m × 3.367 m × 2.446 m (X × Y × Z).

## Results

### Study #1: Single Cell, Small Domain

The maximum cell temperature in Study #1 reached 44°C, considerably higher than what would be expected from a single cell dissipating only 3.92 W. The cause is the limited domain: the warm plume hit the upper boundary before fully developing, curled back down around the cell, and kept reheating the surrounding air. This is a domain sizing artifact included to show why adequate clearance matters in natural convection setups.

No recirculation was observed, confirming the domain was properly sized.

The maximum temperature in Study #2 was 26°C, located in the middle row of the second-from-top stack position. This was initially counterintuitive, as the topmost cells were expected to be the hottest. However, the top cells benefit from the full above-clearance of the domain, allowing their own plumes to rise and dissipate freely. The second-from-top cells, on the other hand, experience the worst combination of conditions: they receive heat carried upward from the cells below, they have reduced above-clearance for their own heat to escape, and they are flanked laterally by other cells that limit the inflow of fresh ambient air.

This behavior is visible in the velocity contour, where the densest cluster of high-velocity warm air (shown in red and orange) is located in the narrow gap between the top and second-from-top rows. In a stacked rack arrangement under natural convection, the hottest cells are therefore not at the geometric top or center, but rather at the horizontally middle position one row below the top.

## Limitations

- Thermal conductivity assumed isotropic at 1 W/m·K (conservative; actual through-plane value applied in all directions)
- Radiation not modeled (estimated 10–20% additional heat rejection at these temperatures)
- Heat generation limited to ohmic (I²R) losses; entropic and side-reaction contributions (~20–30%) excluded
- All cells assumed to discharge identically; cell-to-cell variability not captured
- Steady-state only; warm-up transient and pulse behavior not modeled
- No compression pads, busbars, or end plates; cells treated as standalone solids in open air

## Next Steps

- Introduce forced convection to mimic the airflow of an air-conditioned equipment room.
- Model the cells inside a realistic battery module enclosure, including end plates and compression pads.
- Add cooling fluid flow (cold plate or immersion cooling) to assess active thermal management strategies.
- Run a time-dependent (transient) study to capture warm-up behavior and identify the time required to reach thermal equilibrium.
